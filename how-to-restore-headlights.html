How to restore headlights at home.

A first-timer guide to foggy, yellow headlights: why they haze, how wet sanding works, which pads to use for cutting and polishing, and how to protect the lens so the haze stays gone.

THE SHORT VERSION

  1. 1. Haze is a worn-out UV coat. The plastic underneath oxidizes.
  2. 2. Sanding removes the damaged layer. Three grits, always wet.
  3. 3. Polishing brings back the clarity. The right pad matters as much as the polish.
  4. 4. Protection keeps it clear. Skip it and the haze returns.

Why headlights turn yellow and hazy

Modern headlight lenses are polycarbonate plastic. The factory covers them with a clear UV hardcoat so sunlight does not break the plastic down. Years of sun, heat from the bulb, road grit, and harsh cleaners wear that coat away. The bare plastic then oxidizes and turns yellow, cloudy, or chalky. Fine cracks can form in the surface and hold dirt.

Haze scatters the light leaving the lamp, so the beam looks dimmer and less focused at night. Restoring the lens is a safety fix as much as a cosmetic one.

What restoration does

  • Sanding removes the damaged outer layer of plastic.
  • Compounding and polishing remove the sanding scratches and bring the clarity back.
  • Sealing replaces the UV protection that sanding takes off. This is the step most people skip, and it is why many restored headlights haze again within months.

Can your headlights be restored?

SANDING AND POLISHING FIX

  • Yellowing and haze on the outside of the lens
  • Chalky, oxidized surface
  • Light scratches and road-grit pitting

SANDING AND POLISHING DO NOT FIX

  • Water or condensation inside the housing
  • Haze on the inside of the lens
  • Cracks through the lens

Polishing pads explained

The pad holds the abrasive against the lens. Its material and firmness decide how fast you cut and how clean the finish is. The wrong pad leaves sanding marks behind or adds new haze, no matter how good the compound is.

Cutting, polishing, and finishing pads

PADFEELJOB ON A HEADLIGHT
CuttingFirm, denser foamRemoves the 3000 grit sanding marks with a compound. Leaves a slight haze that needs a polish.
PolishingMedium foamRefines the surface with a polish and brings back clarity.
FinishingSoft foamFinal gloss with a fine polish, or applying a sealant or wax.

Pad colors mean different things from brand to brand. Read the manufacturer's chart before you buy.

Foam, microfiber, and wool

  • Foam. The standard choice for headlights. Firmer foam cuts harder and softer foam finishes finer, so one material covers all three jobs. Foam gives the cleanest finish of the three.
  • Microfiber. Cuts more than foam and less than wool. Fibers can bundle up while you polish, so it suits the cutting stage better than the finishing stage.
  • Wool. The most aggressive option, with the roughest finish and the most mess. It needs a follow-up polish and builds heat. On a thin plastic lens it is rarely the right call.

Open-cell and closed-cell foam

Some pads are sold as open-cell or closed-cell. Sources disagree on how cell structure affects heat and cut, so choose by firmness and the manufacturer's recommended use instead of the cell label.

Pad size and machine

  • Match the pad to the backing plate. A 5 inch pad goes on a 5 inch backing plate, which is what our 5 inch RUPES uses.
  • Know your machine's orbit. DA polishers come with 8mm, 15mm, and 21mm throws. A larger throw covers more area per pass. Detailing forums point first-timers to 8mm or 15mm, and call 21mm a lot for a beginner.
  • Use a smaller backing plate for tight areas. Headlight lenses curve and sit close to trim. Swap the 5 inch plate for a 3 inch plate and a matching 3 inch pad to reach edges, corners, and narrow lenses a large pad cannot. Check that the plate fits your machine, and keep the pad flat and the speed low.
  • Keep the speed low. Plastic builds heat faster than paint. A slow speed and a flat pad keep the lens cool.

Pad care

  • Use a separate pad for each stage. Compound left in a polishing pad scratches the finish.
  • Prime the pad. Work a few drops of product into it before the first pass so the foam does not run dry.
  • Clean the pad as you go. Residue builds up fast. Brush it out or wipe it between sections.
  • Retire worn pads. A glazed, flattened, or torn pad stops cutting evenly.

Sanding grits: the part that looks scary

Sanding feels risky the first time because you are scratching a clear lens on purpose. The process is safe because each grit removes the scratches the one before it made. You are never trying to make the lens look good while you sand. You are trying to make the scratches finer and more even every round.

GRITJOB
1500Removes the oxidation and yellow layer. The lens turns evenly frosted.
2000Removes the 1500 scratches and leaves a finer frost.
3000Leaves a smooth, even haze that compound can clear.

Heavy yellowing or deep pitting may need a coarser start. Some guides begin at 800 to 1000 grit. Whatever grit you start at, move through every grit in order.

  • Keep it wet. Dry sanding builds heat that can cloud or crack polycarbonate, and it clogs the paper.
  • Use light pressure. Let the grit do the work. Heavy pressure cuts deep scratches and thins the lens.
  • Do not skip grits. A deep scratch the next grit cannot reach will show after polishing, and you will start over.
  • Change direction each grit. Sand side to side with one grit and up and down with the next. You can see when the previous scratches are gone.
  • Cover the whole lens. Check the edges and corners. Missed spots stay yellow.

Steps

Follow in order.

  1. Wash and inspect

    Wash and dry the headlight. Check for cracks and for moisture inside the housing. Restoration will not fix either one.

  2. Mask the paint

    Tape around the whole lens with automotive masking tape, covering the paint, trim, and bumper edge. Use two layers. One slipped sanding stroke can scratch clear coat in seconds.

  3. Wet sand with 1500 grit

    Wet the paper and the lens. Sand in one direction with light, even strokes and keep spraying water. Rinse often. The yellow should disappear and the lens should turn evenly frosted.

  4. Wet sand with 2000 grit

    Turn 90 degrees from the 1500 direction. Keep it wet. Sand until the 1500 scratch lines are gone.

  5. Wet sand with 3000 grit

    Turn back to the first direction. When you finish, the lens should look smooth and evenly hazy with no deeper lines.

  6. Rinse and dry

    Rinse off all the sanding slurry and dry the lens. Grit left behind scratches the lens under the polisher.

  7. Compound with a cutting pad

    Prime the pad with compound. Spread the product at low speed, then work a small section with slow, overlapping passes and light pressure. Keep the pad flat on the lens. Stop if the lens gets warm. Switch to a 3 inch backing plate and pad for tight corners and edges. Wipe the residue off and check your progress.

  8. Polish with a polishing pad

    Switch to a clean pad and the polish. Use the same slow, flat, overlapping passes. The lens should now look clear.

  9. Wipe with 70% alcohol

    Dampen a lint-free paper towel with 70% alcohol and wipe the lens. This removes polish oils so the sealant or clear coat bonds. Check the lens under good light. If you still see haze or scratch lines, go back one stage.

  10. Seal the lens

    Apply the sealant or clear coat exactly as the product directs and let it cure. Pull the tape off slowly at an angle once it is safe to do so. See the next section for protection options.

Protect your headlights after restoring them

Sanding removes what was left of the factory UV coat. A bare, freshly polished lens oxidizes again, often within months. Protection is not optional. Pick one of these and apply it the same day, while the lens is clean.

We found no independent, side-by-side durability tests for headlight protection. Treat the ranges below as typical results, not guarantees. Manufacturer claims are marketing until someone tests them.

OPTIONWHAT IT ISHOW LONG IT TYPICALLY LASTS
Liquid waxEasiest to apply. Makes the lens slick and easy to clean.Weeks to a few months. Little real UV protection.
Paint sealantA synthetic polymer layer. Easy to apply and reapply.A few months. Reapply on a schedule.
Ceramic coatingA hard, chemical-resistant layer with better UV resistance than wax or sealant. The layer is much thinner than a factory hardcoat.Months to years, depending on the product and prep. Rock chips and abrasion still wear it.
UV clear coatThe closest DIY option to rebuilding the factory hardcoat. Prep and application matter: runs and orange peel mean sanding and starting over.Longer than wax or sealant when applied well.
Clear paint protection filmAn optically clear film over the lens. It guards against rock chips and abrasion and adds UV protection.Generally the longest. Best installed by a professional.

Whichever you pick, keep the lens out of direct sun when you can, wash it gently, and keep harsh chemicals off it.

Tips

  • Work in the shade. A hot lens dries your product and speeds up heat buildup.
  • Mask twice. It takes two minutes and saves a paint repair.
  • Do both headlights to the same stage. Finishing one lens at a time makes the second one look worse than it is, and it is easy to give one lens more sanding than the other.
  • Check under light between stages. A phone flashlight at an angle shows scratch lines your eye misses.
  • Go back one stage when something looks wrong. Do not try to polish out a scratch that belongs to a coarser grit.
  • Seal the same day. Every day without protection is a day of fresh oxidation.

Questions people ask

How long does headlight restoration last?

It depends on the protection. A restored lens with no UV protection can haze again within weeks to months. A lens with a quality sealant, ceramic coating, clear coat, or film lasts much longer, and you extend that by reapplying on schedule.

Can I restore headlights without sanding?

Toothpaste, bug spray, and some quick kits can brighten light surface haze. They do not remove the damaged layer or replace the UV coat, so the results are usually temporary. Heavy yellowing needs sanding.

Will sanding ruin my headlights?

Not when you keep the lens wet, use light pressure, and move through every grit in order. Each grit removes the previous grit's scratches, so the lens gets finer each round. The risks are heavy pressure, dry sanding, and skipped grits.

What grit should I start with?

In our video we sanded 1500, then 2000, then 3000. Heavily yellowed or pitted lenses may need a coarser start, around 800 to 1000 grit. Move up through every grit after that.

Do I need a DA polisher?

No. You can polish by hand. A DA gives faster, more even results, and its random orbit is the safe type of machine for plastic and paint. Pick pads that match your backing plate.

Why are my headlights cloudy again?

The usual cause is a missing UV protection step. Other causes are sanding that stopped too early or a sealer applied over polish residue. Wipe the lens with alcohol before sealing.

Does this fix water inside the headlight?

No. Moisture inside the housing means a failed seal. Sanding the outside does nothing for it. Have the housing repaired or replaced.
