# Short-form playbook (Kallaway) — reference

Source: Kallaway "Short Form Growth System", Days 1–5 (content games, ideas, hooks, buyer activation funnel, first 3 reps). Distilled and mapped to EAC. Principles only: agents never copy its tone. EAC brand voice (no hype, no exclamation points, active voice) always wins.

Companion to `youtube-engine-playbook.md` (long-form packaging, distribution, A/B). This file covers strategy, ideas, short-form hooks, the views-to-leads funnel, and the first-reps sprint.

Consumers: `research` (idea pipes, mining), `brainstorm` (idea = topic + seed + substance), `content-engine` (hooks, alignment, remix rule), `performance` (on-target vs raw views, conversions per view), `calendar` (first-reps sprint), Content Grader.

## 1. The content game: EAC plays Game #5

Five games exist: mainstream fame, category fame, intuitive products, explanatory products, authority building. Each has different rules; using the wrong playbook wastes months.

EAC sells services (PPF, ceramic, tint, wraps, detailing) to local and regional car owners, so it plays **Game #5: authority building**. Goal: deep trust with people who could become customers, converted off-platform (consultation, quote, DM).

- Optimize for **max on-target views**, not max views. 1,000 views from owners of cars we work on beat 100,000 from anywhere.
- Pure virality is a trap for this game. A viral skit or meme with the wrong audience brings no leads and can drag down the average of the on-target videos that follow.
- Don't chase hit-and-run home runs. Aim for consistent, high-trust hits to the right buyer, each with a next step.
- Exception: the SEMA long-form pilot has a broader, enthusiast audience. Treat it as brand-authority content, still tied to a real build and a real next step.

**Trust = minutes watched × relevance × usefulness**

| Factor | EAC reading |
|--------|-------------|
| Minutes watched | Retention and watch time beat raw reach |
| Relevance | About something a prospective customer cares about (their car, their paint, their decision) |
| Usefulness | Viewer can apply what we taught: choose a tint percentage, know what PPF covers, judge a ceramic claim |

Diagnostic for any piece: would a person who could book us in the next 12 months want to watch this to the end?

## 2. Ideas: topic + seed + substance

- **Topic**: the subject (e.g. "ceramic coating longevity").
- **Seed**: the one-line premise or contrarian take that anchors the video (e.g. "what 5 years of ceramic actually looks like").
- **Substance**: the facts, opinions, and examples that build understanding or emotion (real cars, real numbers).

Outperformance comes down to two questions: is the topic relevant to the ideal viewer, and are the seed and substance interesting or valuable to them? Many people liking a topic is not enough; views from the wrong audience don't convert.

### The six idea pipes (ranked by hit rate)

1. **Our own outliers.** Remake our winners with a slightly different seed or substance. Best source. Check `youtube-catalog.md` and the calendar notes.
2. **Competitor short-form outliers.** Spend 80%+ of research time here. Vet each for relevance to our buyer before remaking.
3. **Other-media outliers.** YouTube long-form, podcasts, forums, newsletters, X. Translate to short-form; validated interest exists.
4. **Category news.** Breaking stories in detailing, PPF, tint, wraps, auto culture.
5. **Competitor non-outliers.** Good idea, poor execution. Riskier.
6. **Originals.** Highest risk, highest reward, no data. Save for later; lowest hit rate until the eye is trained.

Never default to pipe 6. Default to pipe 2.

## 3. Idea mining process (every 2 weeks)

1. **Watchlist.** 10–15 accounts serving our audience (detailing, PPF, ceramic, tint, wrap shops and creators, roughly 10K–1M followers, Instagram, TikTok, YouTube Shorts). Keep the list in the trend brief.
2. **Validate topics.** Review the top 50–100 videos from the last 30 days by outlier score (views ÷ that channel's average). Find the 5–7 topics that recur.
3. **Mine seeds.** For each winner ask: what is the one-line non-obvious takeaway? Do we have something more interesting, different, or better? If yes, that is the new seed. Skip ideas that don't fit our buyer or where we have no real take.
4. **Shortlist.** 10–20 ideas per batch, each as topic + seed, with the link to the original outlier. Log in `docs/marketing/idea-inbox.md` or the trend brief.

No Sandcastles access: use web search and manual review of public pages. Mark outlier scores `est.` when computed by hand, and never invent view counts.

### The remix rule

Keep 8–10 of the "lego bricks" constant (format, hook structure, script structure, visual layout, pacing) and change 1–3 (topic, seed, substance). The more bricks held constant, the better the odds. Remix, don't copy: never reproduce a script word for word or shot for shot.

- Outliers from inside our niche: topics may transfer directly.
- Outliers from outside our niche: borrow the format, hook, script, or visuals; use a topic that fits EAC.
- Example: "3 levels of toothpaste" (basic, moderate, advanced) → "3 levels of PPF coverage" (partial front, full front, full body). Same structure, new topic, seed, and substance.

## 4. Hooks (short-form)

The hook is the headline, intro, and pitch in one. If the viewer leaves at 4 seconds, the rest doesn't exist. Spend 30–40% of creation time on the hook.

### Three components, in order of importance

1. **Text hook**: on-screen text. Eyes land here first.
2. **Visual hook**: what is on screen. Stops the scroll; must reinforce, not confuse.
3. **Spoken hook**: least important of the three. About 85% of short-form is watched without sound. A video can win with no speech; speech over weak text and visuals rarely does.

Draft text and visual first, spoken line last.

### Max alignment principle

All three components must mean the same thing. A mismatch creates a split second of doubt and the viewer leaves. The source reports one creator's two videos with similar production quality differing about 350× in views, with alignment as the missing variable.

EAC example (aligned): text "PPF after 3 years", visual a close-up of the front bumper with no rock chips, spoken "This is a front bumper after three years of PPF. No chips, no yellowing." Same message in all three.

Misaligned (avoid): spoken line about ceramic, visual of a wash bay, text "Detailing tips".

### Two things every hook needs

1. **On-target clarity.** By the end of the first sentence the viewer knows what the video is about and whether it's for them. Write at roughly a 5th-grade reading level, short direct sentences. If the first sentence doesn't tee up the subject, compress it.
2. **Curiosity loop.** Curiosity comes from contrast between what the viewer believes and what we're about to show. The bigger the gap between expectation and reality, the stronger the hook. State the belief, then the contrary reality, then withhold the mechanism until the body.
   - EAC example: "Most people pay for ceramic coating to stop scratches. It doesn't. Here's what it does do." (Belief: it stops scratches. Reality: it doesn't. Open loop: what it does.)

Clarity drives the opt-in. The loop drives the stay-in. Contrast must be true: never manufacture a gap with a claim we can't back up.

### Finding hooks

Hooks in the niche are mostly already written. Find outlier videos, record the text, visual, and spoken hook of each, and remix the structure for our topic.

## 5. Conversion: the buyer activation flow

Views and followers are proxies. The metric that matters is **conversions per view**: how many leads (email signups, booked calls, DMs, quote requests) each video drives. Social is for trust accrual and lead capture, not for selling.

Full flow:

video → comment CTA (single keyword) → auto-DM with the lead magnet link → landing page → email capture → lead magnet delivered → email follow-up → conversion

- Capturing contact info moves a viewer into a higher-trust, higher-conversion environment where later offers are easier.
- A 5K-view video with 50 signups beats a 50K-view video with 5 signups. Make more of what produced leads.
- EAC status: ManyChat automation, email list, and landing pages are not built. Agents may recommend the keyword, DM copy, lead magnet, and landing-page copy, and must say what is `TBD` or not yet live. Build nothing and never imply a flow exists that doesn't.
- EAC is a local service shop. A lead can be an email, a booked consultation, a quote request, or a DM. Pick the lowest-friction capture available; email is the preferred long-term asset.

### Seven lead magnet types

1. Email list home page (newsletter signup): weakest.
2. Single doc download (PDF, Google Doc, checklist): easiest, start here.
3. Educational email course (drip over several days).
4. Free community.
5. Form or waitlist (early access, slot openings).
6. Landing page for a product or tool.
7. Sales page: direct to a purchase or booking page.

EAC candidates (propose; flag which exist): the tint.html comparison tool (type 6), a "PPF vs ceramic: which fits your car" guide (type 2), a pre-appointment prep checklist (type 2), a maintenance guide per service (type 2), a consultation or quote request page (type 7). Never claim an asset exists unless it is in the repo or the user confirms it.

### Choosing the lead magnet: content <> audience <> lead magnet fit

The best lead magnet is closely related to the video that sends people to it. A tint video points to the tint guide or tool; a ceramic video points to the ceramic guide. Mismatch drops uptake. Options, in order of fit:

- The documentation of what the video covered (saves them notes)
- A deep doc on the one piece that causes the most pain
- A full guide, a lighter version of the paid service

At scale: 3–5 lead magnets rotated by content topic, with 2–3 anchors.

### Landing page (keep it simple, 30–45 minutes)

Headline: the outcome they want. Subheadline: specifically what they get. Three bullets: what's inside. One field (email). Button: "Send me the [thing]". A simple live page beats a polished page that doesn't exist.

## 6. The first-3-reps sprint

Preparation mode is procrastination. Make three videos by remixing proven outliers, then learn from real data.

1. Find 5 competitor accounts serving our audience, 10K–1M followers, only 1–2 stages ahead (not mega-creators).
2. Find 3 outlier videos (5×+ that channel's average views) across 3 different formats. Pick formats with the least production friction for us (shop footage, b-roll with text, talking head on the shop floor).
3. Study each outlier: transcript if available, then the text, visual, and spoken hook, and the script structure. Understand why it worked.
4. Remix: hold the format, hook structure, script flow, and visual style; change the idea (topic angle, seed, or substance). Variants: swap the items listed, change the count, narrow to a specific avatar (e.g. owners of a specific car type), or change the examples to ours.
5. Script it beat for beat against the original structure (hook, setup, payoff, CTA), in our own words and facts. Read it aloud; rewrite anything unnatural.
6. Record: about 20 minutes, phone, clean audio over fancy lighting, line-by-line delivery (read a line, look up, deliver).
7. Edit and post: match the outlier's pacing and visual style, add captions. Have the next-step asset (lead magnet or booking link) live before posting.

Early videos won't be great. They just need to exist. The goal is data and skill reps; creative risk comes after the skill floor rises.

Agent role: steps 1–5 are text work. Steps 6–7 are off-system; agents never record, edit, or post.

## 7. The improvement loop (Day 6)

### The 11-point video checklist (one question per "lego brick")

Run it honestly on each video. Score each Pass / Weak / Fail with a one-line reason; if you wouldn't watch it, say so.

1. **Format:** is this the most compelling way to frame the story?
2. **Topic:** do we have data that our ideal buyer cares about this, or only we do?
3. **Seed:** is the angle non-obvious, challenging a belief the buyer holds?
4. **Substance:** can they act on something after watching, or is it only entertainment?
5. **Visual hook:** contrast, movement, something unexpected, or static?
6. **Text hook:** does the on-screen text create curiosity and match the visual and spoken hooks?
7. **Spoken hook:** do the first 3 seconds give on-target clarity plus a curiosity loop?
8. **Story structure:** building anticipation, or listing facts?
9. **CTA:** is there a clear next action, or are viewers left to guess?
10. **Visual layout:** does it suit the people on camera (camera-shy forced into talking head, or the reverse)?
11. **Elements:** do captions, cuts, music, and pacing match the intended vibe, and is it clear what's happening?

### Batch analysis (batches of 10)

Don't judge a video within a day of posting. Wait for 10, then:

1. **Pull data** for all 10: views, follows or subscribers, leads. Rank them as a cohort, best to worst.
2. **Find outliers:** anything 5×+ (10×+ is a clear breakout) vs the batch average on views, follows, or leads. Run the checklist on each. Pattern match: what did winners have that losers lacked?
3. **Form hypotheses** about the next batch ("text hooks are weak: winners had curiosity gaps", "bottom five had off-target topics").
4. **Test one change** across the next 10. Don't fix everything at once. Measure again.

### Triple down

When a video is a 10×+ outlier, make 3–5 more immediately. Hold format, hook structure, and visual style; change only the seed or substance. Two hits on the same combination is a locked formula: run it until it stops working, then go back to experimenting.

EAC adaptation: count only on-target results (Game #5). A 10× views spike with no saves, clicks, or inquiries is a possible wrong-audience hit; don't triple down on it. Long-form YouTube and Shorts are separate cohorts, so don't rank them against each other.

## 8. Expectations and cadence (Day 7)

Volume is the constraint, not time spent. At 4–5 videos/week the source timeline is:

| Phase | Months | Videos | Goal |
|-------|--------|--------|------|
| 1. Skill development | 1–3 | ~50 | Close foundational skill gaps, get enough reps for clean data. Every video feels awkward; any outlier is a bonus |
| 2. Systems | 4–6 | ~50 more | Lock 2–3 winning formulas, test in batches instead of guessing |
| 3. Compounding | 7–12 | ~100 | Scale output, refine the funnel, let trust compound |

Cadence math: 3/week stretches the 6-month path to about 10 months, 2/week to about 16. People lose by quitting when reality misses an expectation, so set expectations to the timeline above, not to one video.

The real accelerator is specific feedback: which single brick to fix next (hooks fine but topic off-target, or ideas strong but the visual hook loses viewers at 0:04). A checklist says what to look at; diagnosis on the actual video says what to change.

EAC adaptation:
- The source's view targets (5–10K, 10–20K, 25–50K qualified views per video) and revenue claims are for a coaching audience. Don't use them as EAC benchmarks. EAC's baseline is its own catalog (`youtube-catalog.md`); judge growth against that and against leads.
- Agent role: when a video underperforms, name the one brick most likely at fault (checklist, section 7) and say what evidence supports it. If the evidence is a single data point, say that rather than guessing.
- Plan around realistic volume. Ask for the user's actual weekly cadence before projecting any timeline.
- The email's Short Form Academy pitch is a sales offer. Ignore it.
